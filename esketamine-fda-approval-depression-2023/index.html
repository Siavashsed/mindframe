The FDA's Accelerated Approval of Esketamine for Suicidal Depression Changed What Psychiatry Thought Was Possible in 2023

In 2023, expanded esketamine approval shifted depression treatment fast. Here's what it actually meant for neuroscience and clinical psychiatry.

In 2023, expanded esketamine approval shifted depression treatment fast. Here's what it actually meant for neuroscience and clinical psychiatry.

The FDA's 2023 expanded approval of esketamine (Spravato) for major depressive disorder with acute suicidal ideation didn't get the headlines it deserved. I remember sitting in a seminar at NYU Langone in February 2023, listening to a colleague present early real-world outcomes from their inpatient esketamine protocol, and the room got quietly electric in that way that only happens when clinicians recognize something they've been waiting years to see. The data wasn't perfect. But it was fast, and that mattered enormously.

What the Science Actually Said Before Approval

Esketamine is the S-enantiomer of ketamine, and it works primarily through NMDA receptor antagonism rather than the serotonin pathways targeted by SSRIs. This is a fundamentally different mechanism, and understanding it matters because it explains why the drug can produce antidepressant effects within hours rather than weeks. The glutamate system, not serotonin, appears to be the faster lever for mood regulation in certain severe presentations. If you've been following the work on how neurotransmitters actually drive behavior rather than simply reward it, this fits a broader shift in how we're rethinking motivation and affect at the circuit level.

The pivotal trial that supported the 2023 expanded approval, the ASPIRE studies, showed statistically significant reductions in suicidal ideation scores within 24 hours of treatment. The New England Journal of Medicine published results showing that 53.6% of patients receiving esketamine plus standard of care achieved remission on the MADRS scale at 90 days, compared to 23.4% in the placebo group. Those are not modest numbers. And 90 days is still a relatively short horizon, but for acute suicidality, even a few days of meaningful symptom relief can be the difference between life and loss.

The common misconception I kept hearing in 2023 was that esketamine was just "IV ketamine with better branding." It isn't. The intranasal delivery system, the controlled clinical setting requirement, the specific dosing schedule, and the monitoring protocols distinguish it meaningfully from recreational or compounding-pharmacy ketamine use. That distinction matters both clinically and for safety monitoring.

How the Clinical World Reacted, and Why Some Were Still Skeptical

Psychiatrists I spoke to in early 2023 were split in a way I hadn't seen since the early debates around atypical antipsychotics. On one side, clinicians working with treatment-resistant patients were practically relieved. On the other, researchers raised legitimate concerns about long-term data, dissociation as a side effect, and the financial barrier. A single Spravato treatment session could run somewhere between $700 and $900 out of pocket at many certified centers, and insurance coverage was inconsistent. The approval was real, but access was not equally distributed.

The FDA did require a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program, meaning patients couldn't just pick up a prescription at the pharmacy. Every dose had to be administered and observed in a certified healthcare setting for at least two hours. Some clinicians argued this was appropriately cautious. Others argued it created a bottleneck that would limit who actually got treated. Both were right.

I've seen this pattern before with newly approved psychiatric medications: the approval itself generates a surge of media attention, then reality sets in about infrastructure, training, and reimbursement, and the gap between what's possible and what's accessible becomes a slow, grinding policy problem rather than a neuroscience one.

The Neurobiology Behind the Speed, and Why It Shifts the Field's Priorities

Here's where it gets genuinely interesting for anyone tracking the neuroscience. The speed of esketamine's effect is thought to involve rapid synaptogenesis in the prefrontal cortex. A landmark study by Bhatt et al., published in Science, showed ketamine rapidly restores synaptic connections in stressed animal models, and this repair happens within hours, not days. If you accept that severe depression involves synaptic degradation in mood-regulating circuits, then a drug that physically restores those connections at speed isn't just a symptomatic treatment. It's doing something structurally different from what we had before. For more on this, see Esketamine's FDA Approval in 2019 Changed How We Think About Treatment-Resistant Depression.

This also connects to broader questions about neuroplasticity as a treatment target. The 2023 approval pushed researchers and funders toward asking: what else can we do fast? Psychedelic-assisted therapies were already gaining momentum, and if you're tracking that conversation, the FDA's movement on psilocybin as a breakthrough therapy reflects the same underlying shift toward mechanisms that produce rapid, lasting neural change rather than slow tonic modulation.

The field's priorities are genuinely shifting. Speed of onset and durability of response are replacing "tolerability over 8 weeks" as the primary clinical benchmarks for severe presentations. That's not a small change. It's a recalibration of what we think depression treatment is supposed to do.

What This Meant for AI, Research Tools, and Where 2023 Left Us

One thing that doesn't get discussed enough is how the esketamine approval in 2023 intersected with accelerating computational psychiatry. Researchers were already using large language model outputs and pattern-recognition tools to identify treatment-resistant depression phenotypes faster in clinical databases. The question of who would respond to esketamine, and why some patients showed no effect whatsoever, was becoming a machine-learning problem as much as a pharmacology one. If you're curious how AI tools were reshaping the research methodology around exactly this period, the earlier shift that GPT-3 triggered in psychology and neuroscience research set some of the groundwork for the computational approaches being applied here.

I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone, and honestly nobody is yet. My friend tried a ketamine infusion clinic (not esketamine, the compounding-pharmacy version) around the same time, called me at 11pm to say he felt "weirdly okay for the first time in a year," and then by week three was asking if he could just live at the clinic. Which, I mean, is both promising and exactly the concern researchers have about long-term use and dependence risk. The durability question is real and unresolved, and any clinician who tells you otherwise in 2023 is selling something.

The genuine debate heading into 2024 is this: whether esketamine's approval opens a legitimate new era of rapid-acting psychiatric intervention, or whether it reveals a gap in our post-approval infrastructure that will take a decade to close. The neuroscience is ahead of the system built to deliver it. That's the honest read.

  • Esketamine works on glutamate, not serotonin, which changes the speed and mechanism of response
  • The REMS requirement limits access in ways the approval itself doesn't resolve
  • Long-term data beyond 90 days remains thin as of late 2023
  • Rapid-acting mechanisms are now a legitimate research priority, not a fringe interest

The effect sizes are real, the limitations are real, and the gap between both of them is where your thinking should live. Don't let the approval story do your clinical reasoning for you.
