The DSM-5-TR Almost Launched in 2020. What Happened Instead Changed Psychiatry.

In 2020, the APA's DSM revision process collided with a pandemic and a mental health reckoning. Here's what it meant for psychology and diagnosis.

The American Psychiatric Association's decision to accelerate work on the DSM-5-TR in 2020 landed in the middle of the worst possible year to be making decisions about what counts as a mental disorder. I remember sitting in on a remote seminar in April of that year, a Thursday afternoon, where a senior clinical psychologist I'd worked with for two years quietly said, 'We're going to be diagnosing half the country with adjustment disorder by December.' She wasn't joking. The DSM-5-TR, the text revision to the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, was being quietly shaped while the population it would eventually describe was under conditions no revision committee had ever accounted for.

What made 2020 so strange for diagnostic psychiatry wasn't just the scale of psychological distress, though that was staggering. It was the collision of two things happening at once: a field already under pressure to modernize its diagnostic categories, and a real-world stress test that was rewriting what 'normal' psychological function even looked like. The DSM-5-TR process forced some genuinely uncomfortable questions about whether our diagnostic frameworks were built for ordinary life or only for it.

What the DSM-5-TR Was Actually Trying to Fix

The original DSM-5 dropped in 2013, and by 2020 it had accumulated a fair amount of criticism. Some of it was fair. The text hadn't kept pace with neuroscience research, the cultural applicability of several diagnoses was being openly contested, and a handful of criteria sets had wording that clinicians found genuinely ambiguous in practice. The text revision process, which the APA formally announced was underway, was not supposed to change diagnostic criteria wholesale. It was meant to update supporting text, incorporate new research, and fix inconsistencies. But the scope of what needed fixing turned out to be larger than the committee initially let on.

One of the areas getting the most attention in 2020 was prolonged grief disorder, which the revision was pushing toward formal inclusion as a diagnosis. This was contentious. Critics argued that codifying grief as a diagnosable condition after a defined timeframe risks pathologizing a normal human process, particularly in a year when mass bereavement was happening in real time. The APA held its position that prolonged grief disorder represented a clinically distinct and treatable condition, separate from typical grief responses. The data supporting this, including Prigerson et al.'s 2009 study in PLOS Medicine, which found that around 10% of bereaved individuals develop clinically impairing grief that persists beyond expected adjustment, was solid enough. But the timing of that argument, in 2020, felt like a live wire.

The revision also addressed cultural concepts of distress more seriously than previous editions. This wasn't cosmetic. Clinicians working across different cultural populations had long flagged that some DSM categories carried implicit assumptions about what psychological suffering looks like that didn't hold across demographics. Updating that language mattered, and the 2020 work reflected it.

Why 2020's Mental Health Data Made Everything More Complicated

You can't talk about diagnostic psychiatry in 2020 without talking about the numbers. By June 2020, the CDC reported that 40.9% of U.S. adults were struggling with at least one adverse mental or behavioral health condition, including symptoms of anxiety disorder, depressive disorder, trauma, and substance use. That's not a marginal uptick. That's nearly half the adult population flagging clinical-level distress within a few months of a crisis starting.

This put the DSM-5-TR revision committee in an awkward spot. The diagnostic thresholds being discussed, the duration criteria, the functional impairment standards, were being set against a backdrop where 'functional impairment' looked completely different depending on whether you'd lost your job, lost a family member, or were simply sitting alone in an apartment with no social contact for three months. I've seen this pattern before in clinical contexts: when the environment itself is the pathogen, our diagnostic tools start measuring the wrong thing. They're designed to detect deviation from a baseline, and 2020 broke the baseline.

If you want the fuller picture of what that mental health crisis did to the field's self-understanding, the piece I'd point you to is this one on how the pandemic forced psychology to reckon with its own assumptions. It covers the institutional side of that reckoning in more depth than I can here.

The Neuroscience Problem Nobody Wanted to Talk About Loudly

Here's something the DSM revision process has never fully solved, and 2020 made it more visible: the manual is categorical, and the brain is not. The Research Domain Criteria framework, RDoC, which the NIMH had been developing since around 2010, explicitly rejects diagnostic categories in favor of dimensions tied to neural circuits and behavior. By 2020, RDoC had produced genuinely interesting research but had not produced a clinically usable alternative to the DSM. Clinicians still needed codes. Insurance companies still needed categories. So the DSM remained the operating system, even as the neuroscience increasingly suggested it was describing the wrong level of analysis.

The specific issue is that conditions like depression, anxiety, and even schizophrenia show enormous heterogeneity at the neural level. Two patients who both meet DSM criteria for major depressive disorder may have almost nothing in common neurobiologically. The NIMH's own RDoC framework is built around this problem. And yet the DSM-5-TR, by design, couldn't fix it. Text revisions don't restructure the epistemology of a field. They update footnotes.

I find the dopamine angle here particularly worth tracing. The way reward circuitry research had evolved by 2020 was quietly undermining some of the pharmacological assumptions baked into DSM-adjacent treatment guidelines. If you haven't looked at what the neuroscience of motivation and prediction error actually says about how dopamine works, the piece on why dopamine drives rather than rewards is a genuinely useful corrective to a lot of pop psychology that was circulating that year.

What the Field Got Right, and What It Got Wrong

My honest read on 2020 and the DSM-5-TR process: the APA got some things right. Moving prolonged grief disorder toward inclusion was scientifically defensible, the research base was there, and having a diagnosable and billable category meant more people could actually access treatment for something that had been undertreated for years. The cultural competency updates were overdue and genuinely improved the text. The speed of the remote review process, pushed faster by the pandemic, didn't visibly damage the rigor of the committee's work.

But here's where I think the field stumbled. There was almost no public-facing communication about what the revision was and wasn't doing. Clinicians were confused. Patients who'd heard about the grief disorder addition were scared they'd be pathologized for mourning. And nobody in the institutional conversation was honestly reckoning with the question of whether a categorically-structured manual could stay relevant as neuroscience moved toward dimensional, circuit-based models. That conversation happened in journals. It didn't happen anywhere that mattered to the people sitting across from clinicians.

Okay, I'm going to be honest here for a second. My friend Dariush, a psychiatry resident at the time, called me sometime around October 2020, fairly stressed, saying he'd just diagnosed three patients in one week with adjustment disorder and genuinely wasn't sure if he was being accurate or just labeling people who were having a completely rational response to an objectively terrible situation. He said, 'I feel like I'm handing out parking tickets at a flood.' I didn't have a great answer for him. I'm not 100% sure the DSM gave him one either, and I think that uncertainty is worth sitting with rather than papering over. There's real debate, not resolved yet, about where appropriate distress ends and diagnosable disorder begins in mass-trauma contexts.

The DSM-5-TR was eventually published in March 2022. But its intellectual shape was formed in 2020. And the choices made that year, under pressure, during a crisis, will affect how millions of people are diagnosed and treated for the next decade. That's not an abstraction. That's the stakes of getting taxonomy right.

The parallel AI conversation was happening simultaneously, and it wasn't disconnected. If you want to understand why some researchers were already asking whether machine learning could outperform categorical diagnosis, the piece on what GPT-3 meant for psychology in 2020 gives you the technical and conceptual context for why that question felt urgent that year.

If you're a clinician, a researcher, or someone who's ever received a psychiatric diagnosis, the most practical thing you can do right now is read the actual rationale documents the APA published for the DSM-5-TR changes, not summaries of summaries. The APA has posted the supporting documentation publicly. The arguments are more nuanced than the headlines suggested in 2020, and understanding where the category edges are, and why they were drawn there, makes you a sharper consumer of any diagnosis you encounter in a clinical chart, a research paper, or your own medical record.

Frequently asked questions

What is the DSM-5-TR and how is it different from the DSM-5?

The DSM-5-TR is a text revision of the DSM-5, published in 2022. It updates supporting text, adds new research, clarifies ambiguous wording, and adds one new diagnosis, prolonged grief disorder. It does not overhaul the core diagnostic criteria of existing conditions.

Why was prolonged grief disorder added to the DSM?

Research, including a 2009 study by Prigerson et al. in PLOS Medicine, found that roughly 10% of bereaved people develop grief-related impairment that persists well beyond typical adjustment and responds differently to treatment than depression. The APA concluded this represented a clinically distinct condition that warranted its own category so clinicians could identify and treat it more precisely.

Did the COVID-19 pandemic affect how the DSM-5-TR was developed?

Not directly in terms of criteria changes, but the pandemic context intensified debates about where normal distress ends and diagnosable disorder begins, particularly for conditions like adjustment disorder and grief. The committee's work continued remotely, and the scale of 2020's mental health crisis made the stakes of diagnostic precision more visible to both researchers and the public.
