One Neuroscientist, Doctored Blots, and Alzheimer's 16-Year Detour

A 2022 Science investigation flagged possible image fraud in a landmark amyloid study. Here is what it means for Alzheimer's research and the amyloid theory.

A single scientist with an eye for tampered images may have just knocked a hole in the biggest theory in Alzheimer's research, and the field is reeling. I read the Science investigation by Charles Piller on a Thursday night in July 2022, sitting on my kitchen floor because I'd run out of couch, and I actually said a rude word out loud. The piece lays out evidence that images in a celebrated 2006 Nature paper, one that helped anchor a specific version of the amyloid hypothesis, appear to have been manipulated.

Here's why your ears should perk up even if you've never heard the word amyloid. This one paper has been cited more than 2,000 times, and the toxic protein blob it described, called amyloid beta star 56, became a tidy story that shaped grants, drug targets, and careers for sixteen years. If the pictures were faked, a lot of very smart people spent a very long time chasing a ghost. That's the gut punch. Now let's talk about what the evidence actually shows, and, just as importantly, what it doesn't.

What Vanderbilt's Matthew Schrag Actually Found

The story starts, oddly, with a failed drug. A neuroscientist and physician named Matthew Schrag was hired to look into simufilam, an experimental Alzheimer's drug, on behalf of investors who suspected the underlying science was shaky. While digging, he started pulling up older foundational papers and running their images through basic forensic tools. What he kept seeing were western blots, the smeary protein bands biologists use as evidence, that looked copied, stretched, or spliced together.

His attention landed on the work of Sylvain Lesne, a researcher at the University of Minnesota and the lead author of that 2006 Nature paper. Schrag flagged what he considered signs of tampering across dozens of images. He was careful, and he said so repeatedly, that spotting a suspicious image is not the same as proving intent to deceive. Image duplication can happen by sloppiness, by honest error, by a grad student grabbing the wrong file at 2 a.m. But the pattern he documented was hard to wave away, and independent image analysts quoted by Science largely agreed the blots looked wrong.

When I first tried to reproduce his approach on a couple of published figures during a methods seminar last year, using nothing fancier than a contrast adjustment, I understood how visible some of these anomalies can be once you know to look. That's the unsettling part. This wasn't hidden in a locked vault. It sat in plain sight for over a decade.

Why the Amyloid Hypothesis Was Already on Thin Ice

To be fair to everyone involved, the amyloid hypothesis is bigger than one paper, and it did not live or die on amyloid beta star 56. The broad idea, that sticky amyloid plaques in the brain drive Alzheimer's, goes back to the early 1990s and rests on genetics, autopsy data, and a mountain of other work. So no, this investigation does not erase the amyloid theory. Anyone telling you that is selling something.

But the theory was already having a rough few years. In 2021 the FDA approved aducanumab, sold as Aduhelm, a drug that clears amyloid, over the objection of its own advisory committee, several of whom resigned. The drug's benefit to actual thinking and memory looked, at best, marginal. Decades of amyloid-clearing drugs have a brutal track record in trials. So the field was already whispering an uncomfortable question. What if we've been aiming at the wrong target, or at least the wrong version of it?

What the 2006 paper did was give that theory a specific, seductive villain. A single soluble protein species that, when injected into young rats, seemed to impair memory directly. It was clean. It was quotable. And that's exactly the kind of result that gets a hypothesis dressed up and sent to more funding committees than it maybe deserved.

The Real Cost Is Measured in Years and Dollars

Let me put a number on the stakes, because abstraction lets us off the hook too easily. Alzheimer's is not rare and it is not gentle. As of 2022, an estimated 6.5 million Americans are living with the disease, and the Alzheimer's Association projects that figure could reach 13 million by 2050. Globally the World Health Organization counts more than 55 million people with dementia, most of it Alzheimer's. Every year without a real treatment is measured in millions of families.

Now stack the money on top. Piller reported that in fiscal year 2021 the National Institutes of Health spent roughly $1.6 billion on projects that mention amyloid, about half its total Alzheimer's funding. That is not proof the money was wasted. Plenty of amyloid research is careful and legitimate. But when one glamorous result nudges a whole field's attention in a particular direction, the opportunity cost is real. The tau researchers, the inflammation people, the vascular and metabolic camps, they all had to fight for oxygen in a room that amyloid dominated.

The scandal here isn't only about one possibly dishonest lab. It's about how a single tidy image can bend the incentives of an entire discipline. This is the same tension psychologists have been chewing on since the replication crisis broke open, and it echoes some of the same soul searching I wrote about around the DSM-5-TR arguments this year. We reward the clean story over the messy truth, and then act surprised when the clean story doesn't hold.

How to Read This News Without Losing Your Mind

I'll be honest, and this is the part where I stop sounding like a tidy explainer. When this broke, my first instinct was doom. My friend who works adjacent to dementia care texted me something like "so has everyone been lying to my grandmother," and I genuinely did not know how to answer for a full day. I sat with it. I re-read the Science piece twice, went down a Twitter hole I'm not proud of, and eventually landed somewhere less dramatic than my 11 p.m. self expected. I'm still not 100 percent sure how much of the 2006 result was load-bearing for the broader theory, and anyone who claims total certainty right now is guessing.

So here's the sober version. Suspected image manipulation in specific figures is serious and deserves formal investigation, which the University of Minnesota has said it will conduct. That is different from the amyloid hypothesis being debunked, which has not happened. Both things can be true. Science is self correcting, but the correction is often slow, awkward, and driven by stubborn individuals like Schrag rather than by the system working smoothly.

If there's a hopeful note, it's this. A field that can be embarrassed in public is a field that's still alive. The blots got caught. That matters.

From Shahnoush

Next time you read a headline about a breakthrough protein, a single molecule that explains everything, do one small thing. Scroll to the funding and check whether independent labs have replicated it, not just cited it. Citations measure popularity. Replication measures truth, and in 2022 that gap just got a lot harder to ignore.

Frequently asked questions

Does the 2022 investigation prove the amyloid theory of Alzheimer's is wrong?

No. It raises serious questions about specific images in one influential 2006 paper about a protein called amyloid beta star 56. The broader amyloid hypothesis rests on genetics, autopsy findings, and many other studies, so it stands or falls on far more than this single result.

Who is Matthew Schrag and why does his opinion carry weight?

Schrag is a neuroscientist and physician at Vanderbilt University who flagged the suspicious images while investigating a separate Alzheimer's drug. Independent image analysts quoted by Science largely agreed the blots showed signs of manipulation, though Schrag himself stressed that a suspect image is not proof of intent.

Was research money wasted because of this paper?

That is the fear, but it is not proven. The NIH spent roughly 1.6 billion dollars in 2021 on amyloid related projects, and this one paper helped steer attention toward a particular target. Even if the images were faked, plenty of amyloid work is legitimate, so the real cost is more about misdirected focus than outright waste.
