Esketamine's FDA Approval in 2019 Changed How We Think About Treatment-Resistant Depression

The 2019 FDA approval of esketamine (Spravato) reshaped depression treatment. Here's what it meant for neurobiology and clinical psychiatry.

What the Clinical Evidence Actually Showed Before Approval

The pivotal trials Janssen submitted to the FDA were genuinely mixed, and I think it's important to be honest about that. The phase III program, known as TRANSFORM, included three short-term studies. Earlier ketamine research published in the New England Journal of Medicine had already set expectations high, but the esketamine trials didn't uniformly clear the bar. One of the three short-term TRANSFORM studies failed to meet its primary endpoint. The FDA approved the drug anyway, citing the totality of evidence and the significant unmet medical need in treatment-resistant populations.

That decision was controversial inside the field. Some researchers felt the approval was premature. Others, myself included, felt the unmet need argument was entirely valid. Treatment-resistant depression affects roughly 30% of people diagnosed with major depressive disorder, according to the National Institute of Mental Health, and that population had been waiting for something new for a very long time. The question wasn't whether esketamine was perfect. It was whether a partially effective option was better than continued failure.

The dissociation and sedation side effects were real and clinically significant. Patients in trials reported perceptual disturbances, dizziness, and nausea with some regularity. The two-hour monitoring window post-dose wasn't bureaucratic caution. It reflected genuine pharmacological risk. That context got lost in a lot of the breathless media coverage in March 2019, which bothered me then and still does.

The Neurobiology Behind the Excitement

Here's why neurobiologists were genuinely excited, separate from the clinical debate. Ketamine and its S-enantiomer esketamine produce rapid synaptic changes through NMDA receptor blockade that appear to stimulate synaptogenesis in prefrontal cortical regions. A landmark 2013 study in Science by Bhatt et al. showed that ketamine rapidly increased spine density on apical dendrites in mouse models of depression, suggesting the drug was doing something structurally restorative rather than just adjusting neurotransmitter tone. That was a fundamentally different model of how an antidepressant could work.

The speed of action was the neurobiological provocation. Classical antidepressants require weeks of sustained receptor adaptation to produce clinical effects. The glutamate hypothesis suggested depression might involve, at least in part, a kind of synaptic pruning or functional collapse in key prefrontal and limbic circuits, and that rapid NMDA antagonism could reverse that within a single administration window. If that model is right, it reframes depression not just as a chemical imbalance but as something closer to a circuit-level structural problem. That's a significant conceptual shift.

I've seen this pattern before in neuroscience, where a mechanistic finding takes years to translate into a stable clinical narrative. In 2019 we were still in the early, excited phase. The long-term structural data in humans was sparse. But the direction of the research was compelling enough that you could feel the center of gravity in depression neurobiology starting to move.

The Access Problem Nobody Was Talking About Loudly Enough

And here's the thing... the approval didn't mean patients could easily get it. The REMS program requirement that treatment happen in certified clinical settings, combined with a list price that Janssen initially set at around $590 to $885 per session (with twice-weekly dosing recommended for the first month), created an immediate access problem. Insurance coverage was uncertain in the weeks and months after approval. Some patients who'd seen the news coverage showed up to appointments asking about Spravato and were turned away because their clinic wasn't yet certified or their insurer hadn't processed coverage determinations.

I'm not 100% sure this was avoidable given the genuine safety monitoring requirements, but honestly, my friend tried to get her mother enrolled in a Spravato program in suburban Ohio in late 2019, and the logistics were a nightmare. The nearest certified provider was forty minutes away, her mother couldn't drive after sessions, and the insurance pre-authorization took six weeks. By the time everything aligned, her mother's psychiatrist had switched treatment strategies entirely. That story isn't unique. It represents a structural gap between regulatory approval and real-world access that the field consistently underestimates.

The certified provider requirement also created geographic inequality from day one. Urban academic medical centers moved quickly. Rural and underserved areas lagged significantly. The FDA's Spravato REMS page laid out the certification requirements clearly, but certification takes institutional resources not every clinic has. That disparity should have been a louder part of the 2019 conversation.

What the 2019 Approval Meant for the Broader Field Going Forward

The esketamine approval did something beyond creating one new treatment option. It gave regulatory legitimacy to the broader psychedelic and dissociative pharmacology research space. Within months of the March 2019 decision, you could see researchers and funders citing it as precedent for psilocybin, MDMA-assisted therapy, and other compounds that act through non-monoamine mechanisms. The FDA had now shown it would consider rapid-acting, mechanism-novel drugs for psychiatric indications if the need was severe enough. That was meaningful institutional signaling.

The Journal of Clinical Psychiatry published several rapid reviews through 2019 examining how to integrate esketamine into existing treatment algorithms, which reflected genuine clinical uncertainty. Where exactly does a drug like this sit? After two failed antidepressants? After four? Alongside ECT or instead of it? Those questions didn't have clean answers in 2019, and they still don't entirely. That ambiguity is honest, and it reflects how early we were in understanding the full clinical profile of NMDA-targeted treatments.

What I'd argue most strongly is this: the 2019 esketamine approval was more significant as a proof of concept than as an immediate clinical solution. It proved the glutamate hypothesis was pharmacologically tractable in humans. It proved the FDA would move on non-monoamine mechanisms under the right evidentiary conditions. And it created a template, however imperfect, for how rapid-acting psychiatric drugs might be approved and monitored. The field needed that template, even if this particular drug's real-world reach remained limited in its first year.

If you're a clinician, researcher, or patient advocate tracking depression pharmacology today, go back and read the original 2019 FDA advisory committee briefing documents for esketamine. They contain a remarkably candid internal debate about how to weigh mixed trial data against unmet need, and that framework is directly relevant to every novel psychiatric drug candidate currently in late-phase trials. The tension documented in those pages isn't historical. It's the exact tension playing out right now with several compounds behind esketamine in the pipeline.
