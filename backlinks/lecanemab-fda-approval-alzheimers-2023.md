What's genuinely new: it's the first drug in a large trial to show that clearing amyloid from the brain can slow Alzheimer's decline. That's a real proof of concept after decades of failed amyloid trials.

What's also true: the slowing was small. On the CDR-SB scale (0 to 18), the difference over 18 months was 0.45 points, about 27% relative slowing. ## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Does the new Alzheimer's drug lecanemab (Leqembi) actually work, and how much does it help?

Short answer: it works in a measurable way, but the effect is small, and it is not a cure.

Lecanemab (Leqembi) is an antibody that targets amyloid, the sticky protein that builds up in the brains of people with Alzheimer's. It received full FDA approval on July 6, 2023, after a large phase 3 trial called Clarity AD.

Here is what the trial showed. Over 18 months, people taking lecanemab declined more slowly than people on placebo. On the CDR-SB, an 18-point scale that combines memory, judgment and daily function, the difference was 0.45 points. In relative terms that is about a 27% slowing. It also cleared amyloid from the brain, which earlier drugs had struggled to show translating into any clinical benefit.

Why that matters: it is the first large trial where removing amyloid was linked to slower decline. That gives researchers real evidence the amyloid pathway is worth targeting.

Why to stay cautious: many clinicians question whether 0.45 points is noticeable to patients or families. The drug is given by IV infusion every two weeks, and it carries a risk of ARIA, which is brain swelling or small bleeds. That is why patients need regular MRI scans, and the risk is higher in people carrying the APOE4 gene variant. It is also expensive, and it is only intended for early-stage disease, not moderate or advanced Alzheimer's.

So the fair summary is this: a genuine scientific step forward, a modest clinical benefit, and a decision that should be made carefully with a neurologist, weighing the benefits against the risks and burdens.
