## LINKEDIN
**Hook:** You can't multitask, and the reason is smaller and more stubborn than most people think.

Your working memory holds roughly four chunks of information at once (Cowan, 2001). That's it. Not seven, and certainly not an entire inbox, a meeting, and a chat thread.

Every time you switch tasks, a few of those chunks get shoved out. What feels like multitasking is really rapid switching, and each switch costs you context you then have to rebuild.

I learned this the hard way. Late one night in a university library, I had a problem set, a lecture at 1.5x speed, Slack, and a half-typed email all open at once. A one-hour job ended at 1:40 a.m., and I retained almost none of the lecture.

The practical takeaway for teams and individuals: protect single-task blocks for work that needs depth, and treat notifications as the interruptions they are. Fewer open loops means fewer chunks to juggle.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why can't the human brain really multitask?

Short answer: what we call multitasking is almost always rapid task-switching, and the limiting factor is working memory.

Working memory is the mental workspace where you hold and manipulate information for the next few seconds. For a long time the textbook figure was "seven, plus or minus two" items. Nelson Cowan's 2001 review argued the real capacity, once you stop people from rehearsing and chunking things, is closer to four chunks. That's a small workspace.

Now think about what happens when you switch from, say, writing an email to answering a chat message. The chunks representing the email (what you wanted to say, the tone, the point you were building toward) have to be displaced to make room for the new task. When you go back, you have to reload them. Researchers call the delay and error cost of this a switch cost, and it adds up quickly, especially when the tasks are both demanding or both use language.

There are real exceptions. You can walk and talk, or fold laundry and listen to music, because one of those tasks is automatic and barely touches working memory. The problem arises when two tasks both need conscious attention, such as reading a report while following a conversation. Then you aren't doing both. You're alternating, and losing a little each time.

I'm a psychology student at York University, and I noticed this in my own study habits. I'd run a lecture at 1.5x, keep Slack open, and draft an email all at once, and a job that should have taken an hour stretched past 1:40 a.m. with poor retention to show for it.

What helps in practice: batch similar tasks, silence notifications during focused work, and write down your open loops so they don't occupy slots in working memory.
