## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Did Neuralink's 2020 pig demo actually prove brain-computer interfaces are ready for humans?

Short answer: no. The demo proved something narrower and still worthwhile.

I'm a psychology student at York University in Toronto, and I watched the August 2020 livestream live. A pig named Gertrude had a wireless implant, and the device picked up neural activity as she explored her pen, with a speaker beeping in time with her neurons. It was a charming demonstration, and it did show that the hardware can record and transmit signals wirelessly from a living animal.

Here is what it didn't show.

First, it didn't show a treatment. Detecting that a snout touched straw is a recording task. Restoring movement, easing depression, or helping someone with paralysis is a much harder problem, because it requires reliably interpreting intention and sending information back into the brain.

Second, it didn't show long-term durability. The brain is a hostile environment for foreign materials. Scar tissue can form around electrodes, signal quality can degrade, and a device that works beautifully for weeks may behave very differently after a year.

Third, it wasn't peer-reviewed evidence. A livestream is a presentation, not a dataset. Researchers had no way to scrutinize methods, sample sizes, or failure rates.

None of this means the technology is a dead end. Academic groups have been developing implanted brain interfaces for decades, and incremental progress is real. But the distance between an animal demo and a safe, approved human therapy is measured in years of trials and regulatory review.
