**Title:** If the FDA's own advisory panel voted against Aduhelm, what does its approval actually tell us about how drugs get evaluated?

I'm a psychology student at York, and I was sitting in the library on June 7, 2021 when the news broke that the FDA had approved aducanumab (Aduhelm), the first new Alzheimer's drug in 18 years. What stuck with me is that the agency's own outside advisers had voted against it.

The approval leaned on a surrogate marker: the drug clearly reduced amyloid plaques on imaging. But reducing amyloid and actually helping someone think, remember, or function are not the same thing, and the clinical evidence for the second part was mixed at best. One trial was stopped for futility, the other later showed a possible benefit after reanalysis, which is a shaky foundation for a decision this big.

To me it looks less like a breakthrough or a mistake and more like a bet. The problem is that the people who pay if it fails (patients, families, Medicare budgets) aren't the ones who placed it.

I'm curious how people here see it. Is accelerated approval on a biomarker reasonable when there's no other treatment, or does it lower the bar for the next drug? And does the amyloid hypothesis deserve this much weight?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why did the FDA approve Aduhelm for Alzheimer's even though its advisory committee voted against it?

The short version: the FDA decided that reducing amyloid plaques in the brain was likely enough to predict a clinical benefit, and it used the accelerated approval pathway to act on that prediction before the proof was in.

Some context. Aducanumab (Aduhelm) was approved on June 7, 2021, the first new Alzheimer's drug in 18 years. Two large phase 3 trials had been run. One was stopped early for futility. The other, after the data were reanalyzed with more patients and higher doses, suggested a modest slowing of decline. That mixed picture is why the FDA's outside advisory committee voted overwhelmingly against approval.

The agency instead leaned on the imaging results. Aduhelm clearly cleared amyloid from the brain, and the FDA argued that this effect was reasonably likely to predict benefit. Accelerated approval exists for exactly this situation: serious disease, few options, and a surrogate marker. The condition is that the company must run a follow-up trial to confirm the real-world benefit.

The controversy is that amyloid reduction and better cognition are not guaranteed to be the same thing. Plenty of drugs have removed plaques without helping patients. Critics also pointed to the original price, the narrowness of the evidence, and the signal of brain swelling and bleeding in some participants.

I'm a psychology student, not a clinician, so I'd treat this as my reading of the public record. My honest take is that it was a bet made with incomplete evidence, and the people who absorb the downside are patients, families, and the health system rather than the decision makers.
