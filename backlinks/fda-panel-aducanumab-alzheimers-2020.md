**Title:** What did the 2020 FDA panel vote against aducanumab actually reveal about how we define 'working' in Alzheimer's treatment?

I keep coming back to the November 6, 2020 FDA advisory committee meeting on aducanumab. The panel voted almost unanimously that the evidence didn't show the drug worked. I watched the livestream as a psychology student, and what struck me wasn't the drug itself. It was how openly the experts disagreed about basic questions.

Two phase 3 trials, EMERGE and ENGAGE, were stopped for futility and then reanalyzed. One showed a modest slowing of decline on one measure, and the other showed nothing. So the argument was about whether one positive trial, plus a reduction in amyloid plaques, was enough. Amyloid reduction is a biomarker, and the panel wasn't convinced it reliably predicts that patients feel or function better.

What I found most interesting is that the debate exposed how little consensus there was on what a meaningful clinical benefit looks like for a slow, degenerative disease. A small difference on a rating scale can mean very different things to a statistician, a clinician and a family caregiver.

Curious how people here think about surrogate endpoints versus functional outcomes in neurodegeneration. Should plaque clearance ever count on its own?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why did the FDA advisory panel vote against aducanumab (Aduhelm) for Alzheimer's disease in 2020?

The short version is that the panel didn't think the evidence showed the drug helped patients, even though it clearly cleared amyloid from the brain.

On November 6, 2020, the FDA's Peripheral and Central Nervous System Drugs Advisory Committee reviewed aducanumab. There were three main problems.

First, the two phase 3 trials, EMERGE and ENGAGE, disagreed. Both were halted in 2019 after a futility analysis suggested they wouldn't succeed. Later, with more data, the sponsor argued that EMERGE showed a modest slowing of cognitive and functional decline in the high-dose group. ENGAGE did not replicate that. Panelists were uneasy about leaning on a single positive trial when its twin was negative, especially after the analysis plan had changed along the way.

Second, the question of the biomarker. Aducanumab reliably reduced amyloid plaques on PET scans. But the amyloid hypothesis, the idea that clearing plaques will preserve thinking and daily function, has a long history of disappointing results. Many experts felt plaque reduction alone wasn't proof of clinical benefit.

Third, safety. A notable share of patients on the higher dose developed ARIA, brain swelling or small bleeds seen on imaging. Most cases were manageable, but it added weight to the risk side of the ledger.

The vote was overwhelmingly negative on whether the data showed efficacy. The FDA later approved the drug anyway in June 2021 under its accelerated approval pathway, which drew sharp criticism and several advisory committee resignations.
