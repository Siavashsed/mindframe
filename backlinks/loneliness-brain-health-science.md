## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Can loneliness actually harm your brain, even if you have plenty of people around you?

Yes, and the key detail is that it's the feeling of loneliness, not the headcount around you, that appears to matter most.

Researchers distinguish between objective social isolation (how many contacts you have) and perceived isolation (whether you feel connected). Studies on ageing and cognition tend to find that the perceived version is the stronger predictor of problems. You can be surrounded by colleagues, family and group chats and still feel profoundly alone, and your body responds to that feeling.

The proposed pathways are fairly consistent. Chronic loneliness is associated with elevated stress hormones, fragmented and lighter sleep, and increased inflammation. Over time these are all things that can affect attention, memory and how well the brain holds up with age. Loneliness also tends to make people more alert to social threat, which can create a loop: you feel alone, you read neutral interactions more negatively, you withdraw, and you feel more alone.

I saw a small version of this myself. As a psychology student at York University in Toronto, I spent eleven days in my apartment during an Omicron lockdown in January 2022, speaking to one delivery driver. My phone had 140 unread messages, yet by day nine I couldn't concentrate on a page of neuroscience reading. Contact was available. Connection wasn't.

That's an anecdote, not evidence, but it matches what the research describes.

What tends to help is quality over quantity: one or two relationships where you feel understood, regular rather than occasional contact, and being the person who reaches out first. If loneliness is persistent or tied to low mood, talking to a clinician is worthwhile.
