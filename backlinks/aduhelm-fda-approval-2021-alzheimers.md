**Title:** When a drug clears amyloid but the trials split on whether patients improve, should the FDA still approve it?

Something about the Aduhelm decision keeps bugging me, and I'm curious how people here read it.

In November 2020, the FDA's own advisory committee voted 10-0, with one member uncertain, against treating Biogen's main positive trial as strong evidence that aducanumab works. Both phase 3 trials, EMERGE and ENGAGE, had been stopped for futility in 2019. A later reanalysis found a modest signal in one trial and nothing in the other. Then in June 2021 the agency approved it anyway, through accelerated approval. The basis was that the drug clears amyloid plaque, which is a biomarker. There was no clear evidence that patients' memory or daily functioning improved.

Three panel members resigned afterward. The list price was about $56,000 a year, and brain swelling and microbleeds (ARIA) showed up in more than a third of patients on the high dose.

I'm not really asking whether amyloid matters. I'm asking whether a surrogate endpoint should carry this much weight when the clinical outcome data split down the middle. If the bar bends here, where does it hold?

**Hook:** The FDA's own expert panel voted 10-0 against Aduhelm. The agency approved it anyway.

In November 2020, an independent advisory committee reviewed Biogen's Alzheimer's drug aducanumab and found no convincing evidence that it slowed cognitive decline. Seven months later, the FDA granted accelerated approval based on the one thing the drug clearly did: reduce amyloid plaque on brain scans.

That difference matters. Clearing a biomarker is not the same as helping someone remember a grandchild's name. The two pivotal trials disagreed, and the agency leaned on the surrogate.

The costs were real: a list price near $56,000 a year, brain swelling in a large share of high-dose patients, and three panel members who resigned in protest.

For anyone who works with evidence, this lesson is uncomfortable. A unanimous expert verdict can be set aside, and the public rarely hears why.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why was the FDA's approval of Aduhelm for Alzheimer's disease so controversial?

Short version: the FDA approved a drug that its own experts said had not been shown to work.

I study psychology at York University and edit a small brain science site, so I followed this one closely. Here is what made it controversial.

1. The trials disagreed. Biogen ran two large phase 3 trials, EMERGE and ENGAGE. Both were stopped in March 2019 because an interim analysis predicted they would fail. Months later, Biogen reanalysed a larger dataset and reported a modest benefit at the high dose in EMERGE and none in ENGAGE. Two near-identical trials gave opposite answers.

2. The advisory panel said no. In November 2020, the FDA's neurology advisory committee voted 10-0, with one member uncertain, that the positive trial could not be treated as strong evidence of effectiveness.

3. The approval rested on a surrogate. The FDA used accelerated approval, based on the drug's ability to reduce amyloid plaque on PET scans. Many earlier anti-amyloid drugs had cleared plaque without helping cognition, so plaque reduction is a shaky stand-in for real benefit.

4. Safety and cost. ARIA, meaning brain swelling or small bleeds seen on MRI, appeared in more than a third of high-dose patients. The launch price was about $56,000 a year. Three panel members resigned.

What came next arguably proved the critics right. Medicare limited coverage to clinical trials in 2022, and Biogen discontinued Aduhelm in 2024.

If you want the full picture, I wrote a longer piece on what the decision says about how regulators weigh hope against evidence: https://siavashsed.github.io/mindframe/aduhelm-fda-approval-2021-alzheimers/

My takeaway is not that amyloid research is worthless. It is that 'it changes a scan' and 'it helps people' are different claims, and they deserve different standards.
