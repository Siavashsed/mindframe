## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What made the FDA approval of Spravato (esketamine) in 2019 such a significant development for treating depression?

The short version: it was the first genuinely new kind of antidepressant in decades, and it works differently from almost everything that came before it.

Most antidepressants, such as SSRIs and SNRIs, act on serotonin and norepinephrine. They help many people, but they typically take weeks to work, and a substantial share of patients don't respond adequately even after trying several. When two or more adequate trials fail, the condition is generally called treatment-resistant depression.

Spravato, a nasal spray form of esketamine, acts on the glutamate system through NMDA receptors. That is a different pathway, and in clinical trials it showed effects within hours to days in some patients, rather than weeks. For someone who has been depressed for years, that difference in speed is significant.

The FDA approved it on March 5, 2019, for adults with treatment-resistant depression, used together with an oral antidepressant. A later approval extended its use to depressive symptoms with acute suicidal thoughts.

There are important caveats, though. Because it can cause sedation, dissociation and increases in blood pressure, it is only given in certified healthcare settings, and patients are monitored for at least two hours afterward and can't drive until the next day. There is also misuse potential, which is part of why the program is tightly controlled. Cost and insurance coverage can be barriers, and long-term data are still developing.

So the significance is twofold: a new mechanism that gives hope to people who ran out of options, and a model of how a rapid-acting treatment can be delivered under safety controls.
