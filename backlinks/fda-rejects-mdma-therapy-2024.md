**Title:** The FDA panel's 9-2 vote against MDMA therapy wasn't really about whether MDMA works. What was it about?

I spent most of a day watching the June 2024 advisory committee hearing, and I came away with a different view than the headlines gave me. The vote wasn't a verdict that MDMA does nothing for PTSD. It was a verdict that the trial evidence couldn't support the claim.

The biggest issue was blinding. Almost everyone in the trials could tell whether they'd gotten MDMA or placebo, because the experience is unmistakable. That makes it very hard to separate the drug's effect from expectation, especially when the outcome is a self-reported symptom score and the therapists administering it also know who got what.

Then there were the safety questions: cardiovascular effects, how adverse events were tracked, and serious concerns about conduct at some trial sites that the panel weighed heavily.

What I find interesting is that the people who wanted approval and the people who voted no were often arguing past each other. One side saw suffering patients with few options. The other saw a data package with real design flaws. Both can be true at once.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why did the FDA advisory panel reject MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD in 2024?

The short version: the panel voted 9 to 2 on June 4, 2024 that the data hadn't shown MDMA-assisted therapy was effective for PTSD, and voted 10 to 1 that its benefits didn't clearly outweigh its risks. That's not the same as saying it doesn't work. It's a statement about the quality of the evidence.

Here are the main reasons, as I understood them after watching the hearing.

1. Functional unblinding. In a gold-standard trial, participants shouldn't know whether they received the drug or a placebo. MDMA's effects are so distinct that most participants figured it out. When people know they got the active drug, expectation can inflate their self-reported improvement, and you can't cleanly separate that from the real effect.

2. Therapist involvement. The therapy is delivered alongside the drug, so it's difficult to tell how much benefit comes from MDMA, how much from the intensive therapy, and how much from the combination. Therapists also knew who received what, which can bias how sessions and outcomes are handled.

3. Safety monitoring. Panel members raised concerns about cardiovascular effects, the potential for abuse, and whether adverse events, including misconduct at some sites, were adequately captured and reported.

4. Durability and generalizability. The trial populations were fairly limited, and questions remained about how long the benefits last.

None of this means the research is a dead end. Many of the panelists said they were sympathetic to the need for new PTSD treatments, and that future trials with better designs could answer these questions properly.
