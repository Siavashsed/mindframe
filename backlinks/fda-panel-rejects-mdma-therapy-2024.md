**Title:** If almost every participant can tell they got MDMA, can a placebo-controlled trial really measure what it claims to?

For a long time I was hopeful about MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD. That made the June 2024 FDA advisory committee votes hard to watch: 9 to 2 that efficacy wasn't shown, and 10 to 1 that the benefits didn't outweigh the risks. I went through the briefing material afterward, and I think the panel's reasoning holds up. The problem was mostly trial design, not MDMA itself.

The biggest issue is functional unblinding. MDMA's effects are hard to miss, so most participants correctly guessed which arm they were in. The primary outcome was clinician-rated symptom change, and many people came in with high expectations. Under those conditions it's very hard to separate the drug effect from the expectancy effect.

Panelists also flagged thin data on euphoric effects, which matters for abuse liability, limited evidence on how long the benefit lasts, and a documented case of therapist misconduct. That last one carries extra weight when the therapy is delivered to someone in an altered state.

None of this proves MDMA can't help. It means the evidence wasn't built to show it yet. Do people here think an active placebo could fix the blinding problem, or is it unsolvable for psychedelics?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why did the FDA advisory panel vote against MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD in 2024?

On June 4, 2024, the FDA's Psychopharmacologic Drugs Advisory Committee voted 9 to 2 that Lykos Therapeutics had not shown MDMA-assisted therapy works for PTSD. It also voted 10 to 1 that the benefits did not outweigh the risks. I used to be optimistic about this treatment, but I think the panel made the right call. Their concerns fell into a few groups.

First, blinding. In a placebo-controlled trial, participants aren't supposed to know which group they're in. MDMA produces strong, recognizable effects, so most participants guessed correctly. The main outcome was clinician-rated PTSD symptoms, and many participants came in expecting the drug to help. Once people know what they received, it becomes very hard to tell how much of the improvement came from the drug and how much from expectation.

Second, missing data. Panelists said the trials didn't systematically record positive effects like euphoria, which matter for judging abuse potential. Long-term follow-up was also limited, so it's unclear how long any benefit lasts.

Third, the therapy itself. The treatment is a drug combined with a specific psychotherapy protocol, so the FDA was effectively being asked to evaluate both. A documented case of therapist misconduct with a participant raised serious questions about safeguards for people treated in a vulnerable, altered state.

In August 2024 the FDA followed the panel and asked for an additional Phase 3 trial instead of approving the treatment.

This doesn't mean MDMA is useless for PTSD. It means the existing studies weren't designed to answer the question cleanly. I go through the vote and the briefing documents in more detail here: https://siavashsed.github.io/mindframe/fda-panel-rejects-mdma-therapy-2024/

For anyone following psychedelic research, the takeaway is that better trial design matters more than more enthusiasm.
