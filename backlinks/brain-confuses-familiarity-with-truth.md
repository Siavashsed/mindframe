## LINKEDIN
**Hook:** Hearing a false claim twice can make it feel truer than a fact you heard once.

Psychologists call this the illusory truth effect, and it is one of the most reliable findings in the field. People rate repeated statements as more true than new ones, even when the repeated statements are false.

The mechanism is simple. Familiar information is easier to process, and the brain reads that ease as a sign of accuracy. Fluency feels like truth.

This is a real risk at work. The idea repeated most often in meetings, decks and Slack threads starts to feel validated, whether or not anyone checked it. Research by Fazio and colleagues found that even people who knew the correct answer drifted toward repeated falsehoods.

One habit helps. Before you repeat a claim, ask where you first learned it and whether you ever checked it. Feeling sure is not the same as having evidence.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why do we believe things just because we have heard them many times?

Because your brain uses a shortcut that usually works and sometimes fails badly. Psychologists call the result the illusory truth effect.

When you meet a claim you've seen before, your brain processes it more easily. It reads faster, it feels smoother, and it fits patterns already stored in memory. Researchers call this processing fluency. Your mind then treats that ease as a signal that the claim is true. Most of the time the shortcut holds up, because things we hear often tend to be true. But fluency only measures how familiar something is, not whether it's accurate, and repetition can make a false claim familiar.

The effect was first shown by Hasher, Goldstein and Toppino in 1977. Participants rated statements they had seen before as more true than new ones. It has been replicated many times since. In 2015, Fazio and colleagues found something even more uncomfortable: repetition increased belief in false statements even when participants had the knowledge to reject them. Knowing better doesn't make you immune.

I learned this the embarrassing way. I once told my roommate, completely sure of myself, that goldfish only remember things for three seconds. She looked it up. Goldfish can remember things for months. I had never checked that claim. I had just heard it enough times that it felt like knowledge. A few habits help:

1. Ask where you first learned something, not just whether it sounds right.
2. Be most suspicious of claims that feel obvious but that you can't source.
3. Treat repetition, especially from a single source, as noise rather than confirmation.

Feeling certain is a feeling. It isn't evidence.
