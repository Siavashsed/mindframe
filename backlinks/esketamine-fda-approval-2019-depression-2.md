**Title:** Esketamine got FDA approval in 2019 and psychiatry has been arguing about it ever since. Is the split justified?

I've been going back over the March 2019 approval of Spravato, the esketamine nasal spray cleared for treatment-resistant depression. I watched the advisory committee livestream that February late at night, and what stuck with me was how divided the room was. Not on whether it works for some people, but on how much the evidence really shows.

A few things I keep coming back to:

1. It's the first antidepressant with a genuinely different mechanism (glutamate/NMDA) in decades, not another monoamine tweak.
2. The short-term trials showed a real effect, but the margin over placebo was modest in some studies, and one of the pivotal trials missed its primary endpoint.
3. Dissociation and blood pressure effects mean it has to be given in a monitored clinic setting, which changes who can realistically access it.
4. Functional unblinding is a real problem. People know when they've had a dissociative drug.

I don't think either camp is wrong so much as answering different questions. Clinicians who see patients with nothing left to try see hope. Methodologists see an effect size that deserves more scrutiny.

**Hook:** The first genuinely new antidepressant mechanism in decades was approved in 2019, and psychiatry split the same afternoon.

On March 5, 2019, the FDA cleared Spravato, an esketamine nasal spray, for treatment-resistant depression. It was a landmark moment, and an instantly contested one.

The case for: it targets the glutamate system rather than serotonin, and it offers a path for patients who have failed multiple conventional treatments.

The case against: modest effect sizes in some trials, the challenge of blinding a drug that causes dissociation, and a monitored-clinic requirement that limits access.

What struck me while researching this is that both sides are reading the same data honestly. The disagreement is less about facts and more about how much uncertainty we accept when the alternative is a patient with no remaining options.

That tension between rigorous evidence and urgent need shows up well beyond psychiatry. It is worth understanding how regulators and clinicians weigh it.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why was esketamine (Spravato) approved by the FDA in 2019, and why were psychiatrists divided over it?

I'm a psychology student at York University in Toronto, and I run MindFrame, where I write about brain science. I followed this approval closely, so here is my honest read.

Why it was approved: Spravato (esketamine nasal spray) was cleared on March 5, 2019 for treatment-resistant depression, meaning depression that hasn't responded to at least two other antidepressants. That is a large group of patients with few options. Esketamine works on the glutamate system through NMDA receptors, a completely different pathway from SSRIs and other drugs that act on serotonin, norepinephrine or dopamine. It can also act within hours to days rather than weeks, which matters a lot for people in acute distress. Regulators weighed that unmet need heavily.

Why psychiatry split:

1. Effect size. In the short-term trials, the benefit over placebo was real but modest in some studies, and not every pivotal trial hit its primary endpoint.

2. Blinding. Esketamine causes dissociation, so many participants could tell whether they got the drug. That can inflate apparent benefit.

3. Safety and access. Because of dissociation, sedation and blood pressure increases, it must be taken under observation in a certified clinic. That limits who can get it and raises cost.

4. Long-term unknowns. Questions about durability, misuse potential and long-term safety were still open.

Neither camp is being irrational. Clinicians treating people who have run out of options see a meaningful tool. Methodologists see evidence that deserves caution. Both can be right at once.
