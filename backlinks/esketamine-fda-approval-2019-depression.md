The March 2019 FDA approval of esketamine (Spravato) represented a genuine paradigm shift in psychiatric treatment, particularly for the estimated 30% of depression patients who don't respond adequately to conventional antidepressants.

Prior to this approval, treatment-resistant depression was managed through either combining SSRIs/SNRIs, augmenting with atypical antipsychotics, or attempting electroconvulsive therapy—all with significant limitations or side effects. Esketamine introduced something fundamentally different: rapid-acting glutamatergic modulation rather than monoamine manipulation.

Clinically, this changed several things. First, response timelines compressed dramatically—ketamine's effects emerge in hours to days rather than weeks, which is transformative for severely depressed patients. Second, the mechanism appealed to a different subset of resistant cases, suggesting that TRD isn't monolithic but involves varied neurobiological subtypes.

The nasal spray format also revolutionized delivery. Unlike oral medications, esketamine requires in-clinic administration and monitoring, fundamentally restructuring how depression treatment gets delivered and monitored.

Beyond efficacy, the approval legitimized ketamine research in psychiatry and opened investigations into other dissociative agents. It demonstrated to pharmaceutical development that innovation beyond SSRIs remained viable, potentially encouraging further research into novel mechanisms.
