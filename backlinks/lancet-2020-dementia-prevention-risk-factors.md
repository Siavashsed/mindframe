**Title:** The Lancet's 40% dementia-prevention figure is real, but what does it actually mean for any one person?

I finally sat down with the 2020 Lancet Commission report on dementia prevention. The headline is that roughly 40% of dementia cases worldwide could, in theory, be prevented or delayed by addressing 12 modifiable risk factors. These include less education early in life, hearing loss, hypertension, obesity, smoking, depression, physical inactivity, diabetes, social isolation, excessive alcohol, traumatic brain injury and air pollution.

The part that stuck with me is how easy it is to misread. The 40% is a population-level estimate. It does not mean that 40% of any one person's risk is in their hands, and it does not mean a healthy lifestyle guarantees you won't get dementia. Genetics and plain bad luck still matter a lot. Some of the risk factors are also tangled up with each other and with wealth, so the percentages can't simply be added together.

I also think it changes the conversation about blame. Hearing loss and early-life education are things many people have little control over, so framing this as personal willpower misses the point. A lot of it is policy.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Can dementia really be prevented, and what did the Lancet Commission say about it?

Partly, and the honest answer is more nuanced than the headlines suggest.

In 2020 the Lancet Commission on dementia prevention, intervention and care estimated that around 40% of dementia cases worldwide could, in theory, be prevented or delayed by addressing 12 modifiable risk factors. Those are: less education early in life, hearing loss, high blood pressure, obesity, smoking, depression, physical inactivity, diabetes, social isolation, excessive alcohol use, traumatic brain injury and air pollution.

There are a few things worth understanding about that number.

First, it's a population-level estimate. It describes what might happen across millions of people if these factors were reduced, not what will happen to you. Someone can do everything right and still develop dementia, and someone else can do everything wrong and not.

Second, the risk factors overlap. Hearing loss, isolation and depression feed into each other, and many are linked to income and access to healthcare. The Commission adjusted for this, but the percentages still aren't something you can just add up on your own.

Third, "prevented or delayed" is doing real work. Delaying onset by even a few years can meaningfully reduce how many people live with dementia and for how long.

Practically, the evidence supports the unglamorous basics: keep blood pressure in check, treat hearing loss, stay physically active, avoid smoking, protect your head, and stay socially connected. None of that is a guarantee, but all of it is worth doing for other reasons too.
