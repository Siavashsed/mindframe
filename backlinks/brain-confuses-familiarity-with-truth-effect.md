**Title:** Why do we believe things more just because we've heard them before, even when we know better?

Last winter I told a friend we only use ten percent of our brains. I'm a psychology student. I've sat through two neuroscience lectures that dismantled that myth, and I said it anyway, in a coffee queue, with total confidence.

That sent me down a rabbit hole on the illusory truth effect. The short version: your brain uses processing fluency as a shortcut. When something feels familiar, it's easier to process, and the brain quietly reads "easy to process" as "probably true." It happens automatically, before any checking takes place.

What surprised me most is that this works even when you know the correct answer. Studies have found that people who knew a statement was false still rated it as more true after repeated exposure. Being smart or well informed doesn't switch the effect off. Repetition doesn't need your agreement, only your attention.

The practical takeaway for me was less "be more skeptical" and more "notice the feeling of familiarity and treat it as a prompt to check, not as evidence."

Has anyone here found a habit that actually helps with this? I'm curious whether anything works beyond slowing down and verifying.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why do we tend to believe things more when we hear them repeatedly, even if they're false?

The phenomenon is called the illusory truth effect, and it's one of the more humbling findings in psychology.

The core mechanism is processing fluency. When you encounter information you've seen before, your brain processes it a little more easily. That ease is felt as familiarity, and the brain uses familiarity as a rough cue for truth. In most of daily life this shortcut works reasonably well, because things we hear often from many sources are more likely to be correct than random claims. The problem is that the brain can't easily tell the difference between "I've heard this because it's true" and "I've heard this because it was repeated."

Two things make it worse. First, it's automatic. It operates before conscious evaluation, so you don't experience it as being persuaded. The claim simply feels right. Second, research suggests it can work even when you have relevant knowledge that contradicts the statement. Knowing the right answer doesn't fully protect you, because the fluency signal and your stored knowledge compete rather than the knowledge cleanly overriding it.

I experienced this myself. I'm a psychology student and I've been taught that the "we only use ten percent of our brain" claim is a myth, yet I repeated it to a friend without a second thought. It had simply been repeated to me so often that it felt true.

What helps? Not willpower alone. A few practical habits:

1. Notice the feeling of "obviously true" and ask where it came from.
2. Separate "I've heard this often" from "I've seen evidence for this."
3. Check claims that you're about to repeat to other people, since repeating them strengthens the effect for you too.
